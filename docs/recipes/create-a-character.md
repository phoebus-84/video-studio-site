# A character people recognise

**Goal:** a recurring presence that feels like the same person across episodes—not a new face with the same name.

A useful character is more than a portrait. It has a job in the story, a way of speaking, a range of expressions and rules for how it shares the frame. Creating that identity is a small design project between you and the agent.

## Ingredients

- A [channel direction](./build-a-channel), or at least an audience and tone.
- A role for the character and a short scene in which to test it.
- Your drawings, photographs or performance references, if you have them and may use them.
- A visual-production route: existing artwork, an illustrator, generated images or an editable 3D character.
- If it speaks: a recording or a speech provider, with the necessary voice rights.

**You leave with:** a character brief, selected visual references, approved voice direction where applicable, an asset pack and a small in-context test. This is an editorial handoff, not a built-in character-training command.

**Costs:** agent work, optional design and voice tests, and any chosen generation. Agree on a small audition budget before making many variants.

## 1. Cast the role before designing the face

Consider a guide for a series about old inventions. The interesting decision is not the colour of their coat. Is this person a patient demonstrator, a curious apprentice, an unreliable enthusiast, or a quiet observer who lets the objects speak?

```text
Help me develop a recurring guide for my series about everyday inventions.
The audience is curious adults. The objects, not the presenter, are the stars.
Propose three roles with different personalities and ways of speaking.
For each, write the same 20-second introduction to a mechanical clock.
Start with writing and references, not final images or voice generation.
```

**The agent proposes:** a role, a few behavioural traits, an example line and the kinds of scenes in which the character would be useful.

**You decide:** the relationship with the viewer. Also define the limits: no jokes during a sensitive story, no pretending to be the historical inventor, no voiceover describing an action that never happens on screen.

Save those choices as the character brief. “Warm and friendly” alone is too vague to guide the next episode.

## 2. Compare visual directions on equal terms

You can provide a sketch, commission an illustrator, license an existing asset, or ask the agent to propose generated candidates. Use only references you may share with the selected provider. For real people, establish consent and the permitted use of their likeness before making synthetic variations.

Ask for a small comparison sheet, with the same pose and framing in each direction. Evaluate:

- recognisable silhouette and proportions;
- face and expression at the size the viewer will actually see;
- clothing that belongs to the channel rather than competing with the subject;
- contrast against your backgrounds;
- suitability for the movement you need to make.

**Example feedback:** “Keep the second silhouette and its restrained expression. The oversized hat hides the specimen when the guide turns. Show the revised version next to a real scene.”

This is more useful than approving a lovely portrait and discovering later that it cannot fit the composition.

## 3. Make a small reference pack

Once a direction is selected, ask the agent to organise the material another person or session will need.

| Keep | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Identity sheet | Silhouette, face, proportions, clothing and fixed colours |
| Expressions and poses | The few actions the series actually needs: listening, pointing, reading, surprise |
| Framing examples | Full figure, medium shot and the small on-screen presentation size |
| Source assets | Original artwork or native scene, clean cut-outs and export settings |
| Usage notes | Where the character belongs; when to hide it; what must not change |
| Provenance and permissions | Creator, reference sources, generation receipts and permitted uses |

Do not make fifty expressions just to fill a sheet. Start with the scenes you will produce. A front-facing still may be enough for a restrained presenter, but it is not an animation-ready rig.

Generated references help guide consistency; they do not guarantee it. A new angle still needs inspection. Full-body animation may require an actual rig, model, illustrator or a different production approach—not simply a longer image prompt.

## 4. Audition the voice with real material

The voice may be yours, an actor’s, an authorised synthetic voice or a suitable preset. Cloning is optional, never an assumed requirement. Only use a real person’s likeness or voice with appropriate permission, including the intended synthetic use.

Choose a short passage containing what the series will actually say: a difficult name, a question, a quiet sentence and a change of emphasis. Compare candidates on the **same words**.

```text
Prepare a small voice audition for the selected character.
Use this exact passage and the voice sources we are authorised to use.
Before any paid synthesis, show the provider, cost estimate and test plan.
Keep each take and its settings. Do not create a new voice clone.
I want to judge intelligibility, pace and character—not just a pleasant timbre.
```

**You listen and choose:** warmth, authority, accent, pronunciation and range. Listen without the image too. A voice that works for a greeting may not carry a seven-minute explanation.

**Keep:** the selected take, role-to-voice mapping, provider-specific settings and pronunciation notes. A visual identity and a voice configuration are separate assets. Changing the speech provider may change the performance even when the role keeps the same name.

## 5. Put the character to work

Test one ordinary scene and one awkward one: a long quotation, a dense diagram, a dark background or another speaker. If the series has both 9:16 and 16:9 outputs, compose a test for each.

Have the agent show you:

1. A still with the longest text and the character in its normal position.
2. A short speaking or gesturing shot at the intended presentation size.
3. An entrance, an exit and a moment when the character is absent.
4. A comparison with the reference sheet after any new generation.

A human review might conclude: “The guide works, but only appears at transitions”; or “Use the same character, with a different reading performance for the recital.” Both are production decisions worth saving.

In *Poesie al museo*, the curator and the reciting performers have different roles. Their placement, appearance and voices were prepared as part of the channel, rather than invented from scratch for each poem.

## 6. Make it reusable without freezing the performance

Keep shared character material at channel level when several series need it; keep format-specific placement and behaviour with the series. Document how episodes select the assets. In a spec-driven format, the theme, kits and release can carry those choices explicitly.

Ask the agent to deliver a simple inventory and a worked example. Suggested documents such as a “character brief” are your project’s materials, not filenames the studio automatically consumes. The channel’s procedure must say how to use them.

When the identity changes, preserve the old pack and make a new version. Do not quietly change an asset that earlier episodes still depend on. Natural variation in a performance is welcome; accidental changes in face, voice or costume are not.

## Acceptance check

Could a collaborator make one new scene with this character without guessing its identity, voice rights or placement? Can you recognise it in a small frame, and does it help the story rather than obscure it?

If yes, you have more than a portrait. You have a member of the cast.
