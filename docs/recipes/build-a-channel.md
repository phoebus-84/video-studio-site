# Build a channel with a point of view

**Goal:** a small creative studio you can return to—not a folder of unrelated videos.

Imagine opening a new session and asking for the next story, rather than explaining the audience, the visual style and every production rule again. That convenience is built beforehand, through a conversation, a few concrete proposals and a pilot you have actually watched.

This is development work. Expect several exchanges, and sometimes several sessions. You bring taste, knowledge and decisions. The agent helps research, compare possibilities, prepare material and turn the choices into reusable instructions and files.

## Ingredients

- A subject you want to return to, and people you want to reach.
- An agent connected to the studio; [installation](../quick-start) can be a separate first step.
- A few references, your own material, or willingness to research them together.
- An initial production budget and a realistic amount of your time.

**You leave with:** a channel brief, selected creative direction, source policy, recurring roles, a proposed series and a plan for its pilot. These are development documents, not a promise that a template already exists.

**Costs:** research and agent time first; image, voice and motion tests only when you choose to make them. There is no need to commission a full set of episodes to decide whether an idea works.

## 1. Find the promise, before the name

“Videos about history” is a subject. “A quiet visit to one historical room, told through the objects left inside” is a promise someone can recognise.

Start with a conversation:

```text
Help me develop a channel, not produce an episode yet.
I want to introduce poetry to adults who feel it was ruined for them at school.
Ask about the audience, the experience I want to create, the material I have,
and what I can sustain over several episodes.

Research a few relevant approaches. Then propose three distinct channel
concepts, explaining what viewers would get and what each would take to make.
Do not generate paid media. Separate evidence from your recommendations.
```

**The agent brings:** a short audience/references review and genuinely different concepts—not three palettes for the same idea. Each proposal should include a sample episode, a reason to return, production demands and likely difficulties.

**You decide:** who this is for, what they should feel or understand, and what the channel will deliberately *not* do. Reject a concept that sounds impressive but leaves you with no desire to make episode two.

**Keep:** a one-page brief. Write the chosen promise, audience, tone, scope and practical limits in language a new collaborator can understand.

## 2. Bring evidence and materials into the conversation

You might contribute a reading list, a folder of photographs, objects you can film, a drawing, recordings, a favourite example or a list of things you dislike. The agent can research the gaps and propose sources.

Ask for an annotated selection rather than a download dump:

- What does each reference teach us—pacing, framing, typography, a teaching method?
- Which material can actually appear in the work, and on what terms?
- What is only an inspiration, not an asset we have permission to reuse?
- What would need to be recorded, commissioned or generated?

Your contribution can change the direction. A box of family photographs may make a more distinctive channel than a plan to generate every image.

## 3. Choose a visual direction you can judge

Ask for two or three treatments of the **same tiny scene**. Keep its subject and message constant so that you are comparing creative choices, not different ideas.

For a poetry channel, a proposal might combine a room, a sample verse, a guide’s position and a small colour/type palette. For a craft channel, it might be a close-up, a maker’s explanation and an annotated detail.

**The agent prepares:** reference boards, rough layouts and, if you authorise the cost, a few original visual tests. Label what is reference material and what is a proposed production asset.

**You choose:** not just “the green one”, but why it works: intimate rather than grand, objects before explanation, room for long quotations, no mascot interrupting the subject.

**Keep:** the selected treatment, rejected directions and the reasons. One beautiful still is not yet a working visual system; test it with a second subject and the longest text you expect to use.

## 4. Decide who appears and who speaks

A channel does not have to have a presenter. If it does, decide the role before the face: host, demonstrator, narrator, reader, comic foil or occasional visitor.

In *Poesie al museo*, a recurring curator helps connect the lessons, while a poem’s reader has a different job. That separation is a creative decision, not a default cast installed with Video Studio.

**You supply or choose:** identity, performance direction, voice permissions, how often the character appears and when they should get out of the way.

**The agent prepares:** proposed identities, a reference pack, voice auditions and tests inside a real layout—not just isolated portraits.

## 5. Give the channel its first series

The channel is the wider promise. A series is a particular recurring product within it.

| Channel-level decision | Series-level decision |
|---|---|
| We bring people into poetry through a museum | A vertical recital presents the poem |
| We use a recognisable guide and consistent source standards | A horizontal lesson explains its language and context |
| Author rooms share a family resemblance | Each format has its own pacing, layout and checks |
| Research and reusable assets live with the channel | Each episode supplies its own text, takes and selected sources |

Start with one series, or an intentionally paired product. Do not open five formats simply because the agent can propose them.

Write a pilot brief together: what it must demonstrate, what can remain rough, what you will review before spending more, and what would make you abandon the direction.

## 6. Watch the pilot, then write the rules

Watch once as a viewer, without stopping. Then review specifics. “It feels slow” is useful feedback; the next conversation should discover whether the cause is a long introduction, empty holds, a flat reading or redundant explanation.

Turn the findings into decisions at the right level:

- **Channel:** “The guide invites the viewer in; never use a classroom lecture tone.”
- **Series:** “A recital preserves the complete text and gives verses time to breathe.”
- **This episode:** “Hold this image until the final word of the stanza.”

Ask the agent to save the decision where future work will read it. A successful conversation that disappears into chat history is not a prepared channel.

## 7. Test the handover

Start a fresh session, or hand the channel to a collaborator. Ask for an outline of the next episode—not a full render.

Can it identify the intended format, the approved direction, the reusable material, the missing research and the next human decision? If you must explain the whole channel again, improve the brief and entry instructions before scaling production.

**Ready to move on when:** you can describe the channel in one sentence; the pilot feels like that promise; the agreed standards are saved; permissions and costs are understood; and the next episode does not depend on remembering this conversation.

The reward is a shorter future prompt with more meaning behind it.
